# The Roof of the World: Mount Everest and Its Base Camps

Mount Everest, known as Sagarmatha in Nepali and Chomolungma in Tibetan, is the highest mountain above sea level on Earth, standing at 8,849 meters (29,032 ft) as most recently surveyed jointly by Nepal and China in 2020. It sits on the border between Nepal's Sagarmatha Zone and China's Tibet Autonomous Region, within the Mahalangur Himal sub-range of the Himalayas. Every attempt on the summit begins at one of two base camps — South Base Camp in Nepal or North Base Camp in Tibet — each the staging ground for a distinct route, a distinct national permitting regime, and a distinct climbing culture.

## Geography and the Two Approaches

South Base Camp sits at approximately 5,364 m (17,598 ft) on the Khumbu Glacier, reached by a multi-day trek from Lukla through Namche Bazaar and Sherpa villages in the Khumbu region. It is the busier and more commercialized of the two, supporting hundreds of tents, a seasonal "seasonal city" of guides, porters, and expedition staff each spring climbing season. From South Base Camp, climbers face the Khumbu Icefall almost immediately — a chaotic, constantly shifting field of house-sized ice seracs and crevasses that must be crossed dozens of times per expedition using aluminum ladders and fixed ropes maintained by the Icefall Doctors, a dedicated Sherpa team.

North Base Camp sits at roughly 5,150 m (16,900 ft) on the Tibetan side, near the Rongbuk Monastery, and is reachable by vehicle — a logistical advantage over the Nepali approach, which requires a trek. The North Side route avoids the Khumbu Icefall entirely but introduces its own hazards, including the notoriously exposed Second Step, a near-vertical rock band above 8,600 m that was long considered one of the route's defining technical obstacles until a fixed ladder, installed by a Chinese expedition in 1975, made it passable for commercial climbers.

Both routes converge, in effect, on the same summit but pass through different intermediate camps — Camps I through IV on the South Col route, and a similar camp sequence on the North Ridge route — with the final push to the summit made through the "death zone" above 8,000 m, where atmospheric oxygen is roughly a third of sea-level pressure and the human body cannot acclimatize, only deteriorate.

## The First Ascent

Mount Everest was first summited on 29 May 1953 by Edmund Hillary, a New Zealand mountaineer, and Tenzing Norgay, a Nepali Sherpa climber, as part of a British expedition led by John Hunt. The ascent followed the South Col route from Nepal and remains the foundational event of Everest mountaineering — the moment the mountain shifted from an unclimbed problem of extreme-altitude physiology and logistics into a proven, if still lethal, objective. Hillary and Norgay's success came after earlier failed attempts, including George Mallory and Andrew Irvine's disappearance high on the North Ridge in 1924, whose fate — did they reach the summit before dying — remains one of mountaineering's enduring open questions. Mallory's body was found in 1999; Irvine's has never been located.

## Commercialization and the Guiding Industry

Everest transitioned from an expedition-style, largely national-team pursuit into a commercial guiding market beginning in the early 1990s, driven by companies including Adventure Consultants and Mountain Madness, both of which became central to the 1996 Everest disaster. That season, a rogue storm caught multiple guided groups near the summit on 10–11 May 1996, killing eight climbers in a single day, including Adventure Consultants founder Rob Hall and Mountain Madness founder Scott Fischer. The disaster, chronicled in journalist Jon Krakauer's book "Into Thin Air," became the defining cautionary narrative about the risks of high commercial climber volume, inexperienced clients, and compressed summit windows on a mountain with almost no margin for delay above 8,000 m.

Despite — or arguably because of — the 1996 disaster's notoriety, commercial guiding on Everest expanded substantially in the following decades. Nepal's Department of Tourism issues climbing permits for the South Side, generating significant government revenue (permit fees plus ancillary trekking and portering economies), and by the late 2010s the number of permits issued each spring season routinely exceeded 300–400, contributing directly to the crowding crises that followed.

## The Sherpa Community

The Sherpa people of the Khumbu region are the operational backbone of Everest climbing on the Nepali side: high-altitude porters, Icefall Doctors, and mountain guides who fix ropes, ferry supplies through the Khumbu Icefall, and frequently summit multiple times per season in direct support of paying clients. Kami Rita Sherpa holds the record for most Everest summits by any individual, having passed 30 summits by the mid-2020s, a figure that dramatizes both Sherpa expertise and the disproportionate occupational risk the community bears — Sherpa climbers have historically suffered a much higher fatality rate on the mountain than international clients, most visibly in the 2014 Khumbu Icefall avalanche, which killed sixteen Nepali guides and porters on 18 April 2014 and triggered a labor action that shut down that season's climbing.

## Disasters and Environmental Shocks

Beyond the 1996 storm and the 2014 icefall avalanche, the 2015 Nepal earthquake (magnitude 7.8, 25 April 2015) triggered an avalanche that struck South Base Camp directly, killing at least 19 people and making it the single deadliest day in Everest's history — a stark reminder that even Base Camp itself, long treated as a place of relative safety, sits inside the mountain's hazard zone. The earthquake also closed the mountain to summit attempts for the remainder of that season.

## Overcrowding and the "Traffic Jam" Crisis

In May 2019, a photograph taken by climber Nirmal Purja showing a queue of roughly 200 climbers strung along a single fixed rope line near the summit went viral worldwide, crystallizing years of concern about overcrowding on narrow summit-day terrain. That season, eleven climbers died, several attributed at least in part to prolonged exposure while waiting in queues within the death zone, where every additional hour without supplemental oxygen sharply increases the risk of high-altitude cerebral and pulmonary edema. The incident intensified calls — largely unheeded — for Nepal to cap the number of permits issued per season or to impose stricter experience prerequisites on climbers.

## Waste, "The World's Highest Garbage Dump," and Cleanup Efforts

Decades of expedition traffic left Everest's upper camps littered with abandoned oxygen canisters, tents, ropes, and human waste, earning the mountain the informal label "the world's highest garbage dump." Nepal's government responded with a $4,000 waste deposit requirement per climber, refunded only if the climber (or their team) brings back a minimum weight of trash, alongside periodic government- and NGO-organized cleanup expeditions that have removed many tons of accumulated debris from the upper mountain and Base Camp since the mid-2010s. Climate change compounds the problem: warming temperatures are melting the Khumbu Glacier and exposing previously buried waste and bodies, while also destabilizing the Khumbu Icefall itself, a factor cited in the decision by some expedition operators to relocate or restructure Camp I/II logistics in recent seasons.

## Records and the Culture of Extremes

Everest has become a magnet for record-setting: youngest summiter, oldest summiter, fastest ascent without supplemental oxygen, and — as noted — most cumulative summits (Kami Rita Sherpa). Reinhold Messner, along with Peter Habeler, achieved the first ascent without supplemental oxygen in 1978, a feat many mountaineers of the era considered physiologically impossible above 8,000 m and which reframed the upper limits of unassisted human performance at extreme altitude. These records sit in tension with the mountain's now-dominant commercial reality: a peak simultaneously pursued as an elite athletic frontier and sold as a guided, fixed-rope, oxygen-assisted package to paying clients with widely varying experience levels — a tension that runs through nearly every modern controversy about who Everest is for and how it should be regulated.
